Photography



As with writing, I felt the instinct to photograph long before I was equipped to do so. I remember the familiar itch I felt as a child when a curved bittersweet branch or a soft patch of melting snow caught my eye: I wanted desperately to freeze the image and imbue it with the feeling in my chest, to make what the scene meant to me legible to others. Satisfying this impulse at the time usually meant taking a picture with my pocket-sized digital camera or scribbling out a few sentences describing what I saw. In college, I started printing the images I took and writing long, meandering captions, hoarding these little fragments away in my journal. In my last semester of college I finally had room in my schedule to take a studio photography class. So now that I’m equipped, I want to share what I’ve come up with.


I’m obviously not the first to have noticed the relationship between writing and photography. Many of my favorite artists are both writers and photographers (Sophie Calle, Patti Smith). There were more English majors than art majors in my photography class. What is it, exactly, that makes writing and photographing two forms of the same impulse? They are both ways of documenting and telling stories, but they’re also both a way of taking in and digesting the world around us. In the opening of her essay “In Plato’s Cave,” Susan Sontag writes, “[photographs] are a grammar and, even more importantly, an ethics of seeing.” According to Sontag, photographing is an imposition of socio-linguistic structures onto what our senses gather. On the most basic level, “they document, interpret, and perhaps most essentially, define the photographer’s position to its subject.” Photography is ideology, power, and culture replicated and reinforced. It is, therefore, an excellent medium for exploring the limits of The Subject, the concern of utmost importance in literary theory.


A photographic practice is, for me, a way of tuning in to the ethics of seeing, of re/defining who I am in relation to my subjects, and of letting them re/define themselves. You’ll be unsurprised to learn that I am most interested in portraiture. I look for moments that reflect, sparkle, and charm; moments of intimacy, candor, wonder. My photos are interpretations of spirit. All of the images here I processed and printed myself in the Smith College darkrooms. I used a Nikon F3 and Kentmere 400 Pan B&W film. I just bought myself a Canon AE-3—more coming soon.
